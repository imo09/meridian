Part 1 - In short

Overview

English has no single future tense: the form depends on how sure, how planned and how far along the action is. To choose one, ask:

  • Fixed timetable or programme?(present simple) The train leaves at 9.
  • Arranged, with time, date or place?(present continuous) What are you doing this evening? - I am staying at home.
  • Planned before now, or a sign you can see now?(going to) Look at those grey clouds! It's going to rain!
  • Opinion, decision made now, offer or promise?(will) I think it will rain tomorrow.
  • In progress at an exact future time?(future continuous) At this time tomorrow, I will be teaching.
  • Finished before a future point?(future perfect) By the time we get there, the play will have begun.
  • How long, measured at a future point?(future perfect continuous) By next July, I will have been teaching for twenty years.
FormTimelineStructureExample
Present simplesubject + V1 (+ s)The train leaves at 9.
Present continuoussubject + am/is/are + V-ingI am meeting her this evening.
be going tosubject + am/is/are going to + V1I am going to move to Italy next month.
Future simple (will)subject + will + V1I think you will enjoy this film.
Future continuoussubject + will be + V-ingI will be having dinner at 7 pm.
Future perfectsubject + will have + V3I will have finished my homework by the time you get back.
Future perfect continuoussubject + will have been + V-ingBy the time she retires, she will have been teaching for forty years.

Key points

  • Time clausesAfter if, when, as soon as, before, after, until, unless, while, once use the present simple, not will: I'll say hi as soon as I see her.
  • by the timeBy the time + present simple takes the future perfect; by the time + past simple takes the past perfect: By the time we got there, the play had already begun.
  • will or going toFor a prediction based on what you see now, use going to (strong); for an opinion or belief, use will (weak), often after I think, I hope, probably. When both readings fit, both forms are correct.
  • Present continuous as a futureIt needs a future time signal (tomorrow, later, next week, this evening); without one it means now. With the signal, am teaching and am going to teach often mean the same.
  • In progress or finishedThe future continuous shows the action going on at the future moment; the future perfect shows it already finished by then: the baby will be sleeping when you call; the workers will have finished by the time I arrive.
  • The perfect familyhave/has + V3 looks back from now, had + V3 from a past moment, will have + V3 from a future moment. The partner tense marks the moment: past perfect with past simple, present perfect with the present, future perfect with a present simple time clause or by + a future time.
  • State verbsknow, be, own, love have no continuous form, also in the perfect: By 2030 we will have known each other for 30 years (not will have been knowing).

V1 = base form, V3 = past participle, V-ing = -ing form. Short forms: will = 'll, will not = won't.

Part 2

Tenses

2.1Present forms for the future

Two present tenses also talk about the future: the present simple for fixed timetables and inside time clauses, the present continuous for personal arrangements.

Present simple

Future events fixed by a timetable or programme, and every future action inside a time or condition clause.

FormStructureExample
Positivesubject + V1 (+ s)The train leaves at 9.
Negativesubject + don't/doesn't + V1The shop doesn't open on Sunday.
Question(Wh-) + do/does + subject + V1?What time does the next train arrive?
  • Timetable or programmeThe train leaves at 9. If you want to go to the concert, it starts at 8 pm. Don't be late.
  • Time clause (when, as soon as, before, after, until)I'll buy you a newspaper when I go out. Stay in your seat until the bell rings.
  • Condition clause (if, unless, as long as)If I see her, I will say hi. I will help you move if I get here on time.
  • Watch outNever will inside the time or condition clause: ✗ as soon as I will see her ✓ as soon as I see her.

Present continuous

Future plans and (social) arrangements already fixed: we usually give the time, the date and/or the place.

FormStructureExample
Positivesubject + am/is/are + V-ingI am meeting John at the airport tomorrow at 5.30.
Negativesubject + am/is/are not + V-ingI am not working tomorrow, so we can go to the beach.
Question(Wh-) + am/is/are + subject + V-ing?What are you doing this evening?
  • Arrangement (time, date, place)What are you doing this evening? - I am staying at home. Ann and Andy are getting married on the 14th of June.
  • Watch outWithout a future time signal the present continuous means now.
I am teaching.Right now.
I am teaching tomorrow.An arrangement for tomorrow: same meaning as I am going to teach tomorrow.

Signal words

SignalFormExample
at 9, at 8 pm, timetable, programmePresent simpleThe train leaves at 9.
if, when, as soon as, before, after, until, unless, while, once, as long asPresent simple (in the clause)We will go out as soon as we finish this.
tomorrow, later, this evening, next week, next month, on June 25thPresent continuousWe are getting married on June 25th.

Future time signals such as tomorrow or next week go with every future form: they matter most with the present continuous, because they turn it from now into future.

2.2will and going to

Both talk about predictions and plans: going to when the future is already visible or planned, will when it is an opinion or a decision made now.

be going to

Intentions decided before the moment of speaking, and predictions based on signs we can see now.

FormStructureExample
Positivesubject + am/is/are going to + V1I am going to move to Italy next month.
Negativesubject + am/is/are not going to + V1He isn't going to pass if he doesn't study.
Question(Wh-) + am/is/are + subject + going to + V1?What are you going to do when you leave university?
  • Plan or intention made before nowI am going to move to Italy next month. We are going to spend our holidays in France next year.
  • Prediction from evidence nowIt's getting late. You are going to miss your train. The water is already very hot. It is going to boil in a minute.

Future simple (will)

Opinions about the future, decisions taken at the moment of speaking, future facts, offers, promises, threats and requests.

FormStructureExample
Positivesubject + will ('ll) + V1Perhaps I will see you tomorrow.
Negativesubject + will not (won't) + V1I don't think the exam will be very difficult.
Question(Wh-) + will + subject + V1?What will happen if the firm gets reorganised?
  • Opinion or beliefI think you will enjoy this film. I hope the weather will be nice when you get to Sardinia.
  • Decision at the moment of speakingWe seem to be lost. I'll stop and ask for the way home. Put your wallet away. I will pay for the tickets.
  • Future factHe will be 50 next month.
  • Offer, promise, threat, requestI will be careful, I promise. Stop that, or I'll tell your parents. Will you get me some fruit?
  • First conditionalI will visit you later if I have the time.
I think it will rain tomorrow.Weak prediction: an opinion.
Look at those grey clouds! It's going to rain!Strong prediction: the evidence is there now.
I am going to move to Italy.Decided before: a plan.
I forgot the car keys. - Don't worry, I will get them for you.Decided now: an offer.

Signal words

SignalFormExample
Look!, visible evidence, It's getting lategoing toTed looks very tired. He is going to fall asleep soon.
a plan already made (next month, next year)going toPaul is going to learn French.
think, believe, expect, hope, be sure, be afraid, probably, perhaps, possibly, certainly, I supposewillI suppose real estate prices will go up again next year.
I promise, or (threat), Will you ...? (request)willGive me all your money or I will shoot you.
if + present simplewill (main clause)You will burn yourself if you touch the stove.

With I think both forms are possible when there is some evidence: Take the umbrella. I think it will rain / it is going to rain in the afternoon.

2.3Future continuous and future perfect

All three forms use will and look at a precise moment in the future: is the action going on at that moment, already finished, or how long has it lasted?

Future continuous

An action that will be in progress at an exact time in the future.

FormStructureExample
Positivesubject + will be + V-ingI will be teaching at 8:00 pm tomorrow.
Negativesubject + won't be + V-ingDon't call at 6.00. I won't be answering: I will be doing my homework then.
Question(Wh-) + will + subject + be + V-ing?Where will you be working in ten years' time?
  • In progress at a precise future timeAt this time tomorrow, I will be teaching. This time next week he will be camping in the mountains.
  • Going on when another action happensThe baby will be sleeping when you call Mrs Stevenson.
  • Already planned, or part of a routineI will be driving into town later on. Can I give you a lift?
  • Polite question about plansWill you be going out this evening? Will you be using your car at the weekend?
At this time tomorrow, I will be teaching.Future continuous: in progress at a future moment.
At this time yesterday, I was teaching.Past continuous: the same idea at a past moment.

Future perfect

An action that will be completed at some point before a fixed moment in the future.

FormStructureExample
Positivesubject + will have + V3I will have finished my homework by next Wednesday.
Negativesubject + won't have + V3You won't have lost enough weight to fit into those trousers by next week.
Question(Wh-) + will + subject + have + V3?Will you have finished baking the cake before the guests arrive?
  • Completed before a future pointBy the time we get there, the play will have begun. I haven't finished my homework yet, but I will have finished it by next Wednesday.
  • How much or how many by thenBy the end of next year he will have planted 2,000 trees. When he reaches Land's End, he will have walked 1,500 miles.
  • State up to a future point (state verbs)By 2030 we will have known each other for 30 years. By next Christmas, they will have been married for twenty-five years.

Future perfect continuous

The length of an activity, measured at a point in the future.

FormStructureExample
Positivesubject + will have been + V-ingBy next July, I will have been teaching for twenty years.
Negativesubject + won't have been + V-ingBy Friday he won't have been working here for a month.
QuestionHow long + will + subject + have been + V-ing?How long will you have been living here by then?
  • Duration up to a future pointBy the time she retires, she will have been teaching for forty years. By midnight, we will have been travelling for twelve hours.
By midnight, she will have been studying for ten hours.The activity and its length.
By the end of the day, I will have written twenty emails.The result: what is finished, or how many.

Signal words

SignalFormExample
at this time tomorrow, this time next week, at 8 pm tomorrow, thenFuture continuousThis time next week I will be lying on the beach in Australia.
by + future time, by the time + present simple, by the end of, by the age of, in a week's time, in ten years' timeFuture perfectIn ten years' time, everyone will have forgotten about his book.
by + future time + for + lengthFuture perfect continuousThis time next month, he will have been working on the same project for a year.

This time next month + for a year asks for the future perfect continuous, while this time next month alone asks for the future continuous: the length of time decides.

One question, five futures

The same question can be asked with every future form; the meaning changes only slightly.

  • What time does the next train arrive? (timetable)
  • What time is the next train arriving? (arrangement)
  • What time is the next train going to arrive? (plan or expectation)
  • What time will the next train arrive? (neutral future)
  • What time will the next train be arriving? (in progress or planned, very polite)

2.4Time clauses and by the time

A sentence about the future often has two parts: the main action, with a future form, and a time clause, with a present form.

After if, when, as soon as, before, after, until, unless, while and once, use the present simple even when the meaning is future.

OrderStructureExample
Time clause firsttime word + subject + V1(s), subject + will + V1As soon as I see her, I will say hi.
Main clause firstsubject + will + V1 + time word + subject + V1(s)I will say hi as soon as I see her.

With the time clause first, a comma separates the two parts. To stress that the first action is finished, the time clause can use the present perfect: I'll get down to work after I have written this e-mail.

✗ If I will see her, I will say hi.No will in the time or condition clause.
✓ If I see her, I will say hi.Present simple in the clause, will in the main part.

by the time

By the time fixes the reference point; the tense of the clause tells you which perfect to use in the main part.

  • by the time + present simpleBy the time we get there, the play will have begun. By the time I arrive home, the workers will have finished repairing my TV set.
  • by the time + past simpleBy the time we got there, the play had already begun. By the time I got to the restaurant, it had already closed.

Signal words

SignalFormExample
when, as soon as, before, after, until, while, oncePresent simple in the clause, will in the main partI will buy him one when I go downtown.
if, unless, as long asPresent simple in the clause, will in the main partIt's freezing outside. You will get a cold if you go out.
by the time + present simpleFuture perfectBy the time you arrive, I will have finished my homework.
by the time + past simplePast perfectBy the time you called, I had already recovered from the accident.

2.5The perfect family

The six perfect tenses share have + V3: each one looks back from a reference point (now, a past moment or a future moment) at something that happened or lasted before it.

Past perfect with the past simple, present perfect with the present, future perfect with a present simple time clause or by + a future time.

TenseTimelineStructureExample
Present perfectsubject + have/has (haven't/hasn't) + V3She has lost her keys, so she can't get into the house.
Present perfect continuoussubject + have/has (haven't/hasn't) been + V-ingI have been waiting for over an hour and the bus still hasn't come.
Past perfectsubject + had (hadn't) + V3When I got to the cinema, the film had already started.
Past perfect continuoussubject + had (hadn't) been + V-ingSarah was tired because she had been studying all night.
Future perfectsubject + will/won't have + V3By the time you arrive, I will have finished my homework.
Future perfect continuoussubject + will/won't have been + V-ingBy midnight, we will have been travelling for twelve hours.

Questions put the first auxiliary before the subject: Have you ever watched a thriller? Had they heard about the film? Will you have finished by then? How long have you been learning English?

Present perfect and present perfect continuous

From now, looking back. Full rules in Lesson 02; here the uses that appear in this lesson's exercises.

  • Experience, time not statedHave you ever watched a thriller? We have never ever heard anything like this before.
  • Result in the presentShe has lost her keys, so she can't get into the house.
  • Recent news (just, already, yet)Hooray! I have just won the jackpot! He has not finished his report yet.
  • Time not over yetI have read three books this month.
  • State up to now (for, since)They have known each other since primary school. He has worked here since 2022.
  • Activity up to now, with its lengthI have been waiting for over an hour and the bus still hasn't come. How long have you been learning English?
  • Activity with a visible result nowLook! The children are covered in paint. They have been decorating the room. He is out of breath because he has been running.
  • Recent repeated activityHe has been practising the guitar every day recently, and he is improving quickly.

Past perfect and past perfect continuous

From a past moment, looking further back. Full rules in Lesson 01.

  • Completed before a past momentWe got to the station late, so the train had already left. Jane had already left her husband when she met Josh.
  • Experience up to a past momentTom had never been abroad before last summer. Jimmy had never been to a concert until last week.
  • Duration before a past momentBy the time he came home, the children had been sleeping for hours. They had been learning English for 4 years when they first got the opportunity to visit England.
  • Cause of a past stateThe ground was wet because it had been raining. I was hungry because I had not eaten all day.

Signal words

SignalFormExample
yet, already, just, recently, ever, never, since, for, this week/month/yearPresent perfectShe has not finished her book yet.
for, since, all day, all morning, recently, how longPresent perfect continuousI have been studying all afternoon.
before, after, already, when + past event, by the time + past eventPast perfectThe train had left before we arrived.
for hours, all day/night, because, tired / exhausted, before + past eventPast perfect continuousShe had been working all day, so she was tired.
by + future time, by next week/month/year, by the age of, by the end of, by the time + present simpleFuture perfectThey will have completed the project by Friday.
by + future time + for, by the time ... forFuture perfect continuousBy next July, I will have been teaching for twenty years.

for, since, recently and all day appear in more than one row: the simple form gives the result or the number (I have read three books), the continuous form gives the activity and its length (I have been reading all day). With state verbs only the simple form is possible.

Watch out

  • State verbs (know, be, own, love) stay simple in every perfect tense: How long have you known your best friend? By next Christmas they will have been married for twenty-five years.
  • Two past actions going on at the same time take the past continuous, not a perfect tense: I was reading while she was seeing the doctor.
  • With before or after the order of two past actions is already clear, so the past simple is often possible too: Before I left for school, I had checked / I checked my mail.
